Interpretation of the 18 unexpected acceptances
3 freshness/completeness expectations: rollback to empty, truncation to a valid
historical prefix, and a backdated expired transition. The last two original
failure families are reproduced. A public-key proof of a presented historical
chain cannot by itself prove that the chain is the latest one or that the
notary's timestamps describe real commitment time. Trusted external temporal
and latest-head evidence are needed for those stronger claims.

2 individual-identifier replay expectations: genuine test-authority signatures,
correct current predecessors, but either a reused nonce with a different
determination ID, or a reused determination ID with a different nonce.
The core refuses both with 'authorization replay'. The judge accepts both.
Cause in supplied source: core rejects nonce OR determination reuse; judge
tracks only the tuple (nonce, determination_id). This is a verified mismatch
between engine policy and outside verification. The fixtures model an authority
issuing reused identifiers; no attacker signing privilege is assumed.

13 record-schema/metadata expectations: accepted changes or omissions include
sequence, event, previous-record hash, missing state/state_digest, and numeric
timestamp shape. These violate the stricter expanded head/record expectations.
They are not 13 demonstrated unauthorized constitutional transitions.
The judge's declared contract is narrower: every presented transition was
authorized by the public key. Some metadata is not authenticated by those
envelopes, and the contract needs to specify which fields are mandatory.

12 ERROR scenarios: malformed records raise uncaught KeyError, TypeError, or
AttributeError instead of returning a clean rejection. This is a demonstrated
input-handling weakness. Whether it becomes denial of service or another risk
depends on the caller's exception handling; exceptions are not credited as holds.
